Scouting America’s Foundation

Understanding Scouting America’s mission and methods helps families appreciate the comprehensive youth development program your Scout will experience.

Mission Statement

The mission of Scouting America is to prepare young people to make ethical and moral choices over their lifetimes by instilling in them the values of the Scout Oath and Law.

Vision Statement

Scouting America will prepare every eligible youth in America to become a responsible, participating citizen and leader who is guided by the Scout Oath and Law.

The Scout Oath

On my honor I will do my best To do my duty to God and my country and to obey the Scout Law; To help other people at all times; To keep myself physically strong, mentally awake, and morally straight.

The Scout Law

A Scout Is: Trustworthy, Loyal, Helpful, Friendly, Courteous, Kind, Obedient, Cheerful, Thrifty, Brave, Clean, Reverent

Scouting’s Aims and Methods

Scouting achieves its mission through four main aims: character development, leadership development, citizenship training, and personal fitness. These are accomplished through eight interconnected methods:

  1. Ideals - Living by the Scout Oath, Law, motto, and slogan
  2. Patrols - Small group experiences that teach responsibility and citizenship
  3. Outdoor Programs - Learning through nature and outdoor adventures
  4. Advancement - Progressive challenges that build self-confidence and skills
  5. Association with Adults - Positive role modeling and mentoring relationships
  6. Personal Growth - Individual development through service and reflection
  7. Leadership Development - Learning and practicing leadership skills
  8. Uniform - Visible commitment to Scouting ideals and community service

Getting Started - Parents Information

Welcome to Scouting America Troop 500G! We are happy to have you and your daughter join us for the awesome adventure of Scouting!

Scouting is a new experience for both scout and parent. There is a time when the parents, although still involved in new ways, take a step back and let their daughters take on more and more responsibility for their achievements. While Scouting is girl-led, it is parent-facilitated. Your role as a facilitator requires a commitment of your time on both organizational and activity levels. This guide provides what a parent needs to know to survive the first year of scout life.

Your First Meeting

We meet Monday evenings at Advent Lutheran Church (regular meetings 7:00-8:30 PM, summer meetings 6:30-8:00 PM). All activities are tracked in Scoutbook and Google Calendar, and we organize monthly outings for advancement and merit badges.

You may not have everything we list as required when you start - don’t worry! Do your best, show up ready to learn about Scouting, and we’ll work with you on the rest. More detailed information about meetings and activities can be found later in the handbook.

Troop Communications

We use GroupMe for routine chats following all Youth Protection Training two-deep policies. We have groups for the troop, committee, parents, and each patrol, as well as groups for specific events as needed. We use Scoutbook as much as possible to keep all information in one place. Adult leaders also use a Google Drive, but we are working to move as much information as possible onto the website troop500.org to reduce the number of locations we need to access.

Joining Conference

Every new Scout family is required to participate in a joining conference with troop leadership before their Scout’s first overnight camping experience. This friendly, informative meeting helps us understand your Scout’s unique strengths, interests, and any special considerations that will help ensure their success in our program.

The joining conference is scheduled within the first month after joining and serves as an important step in creating a supportive environment where every Scout can thrive. During the conference, we’ll discuss how we can work together as partners to provide the best possible Scouting experience and establish open communication.

For detailed information about what to expect and how to prepare, see our comprehensive Conference Communications section.

Welcome to your Scouting adventure! Your Scout rank is your introduction to Scouting and the foundation for everything that follows. This rank should be completed within your first month and teaches you the fundamentals you need to participate fully in troop activities.

Your First 30 Days: What to Expect

Starting Scouting is an exciting adventure! Here’s what you can expect during your first month as you work toward earning your Scout rank.

Your First Meeting Experience

When you walk into your first troop meeting, you’ll be welcomed by your new Scout family:

Meeting Your Patrol

  • You’ll be assigned to a patrol - your small group of fellow Scouts
  • Your patrol leader (an experienced Scout) will help guide you through your first meetings
  • Patrol members will become some of your closest friends in Scouting
  • You’ll participate in patrol activities, games, and skill-building exercises

Learning the Basics

  • Practice the Scout Oath, Law, Motto, and Slogan
  • Learn the Scout sign, salute, and handshake
  • Understand how the patrol method works
  • Get familiar with your Scout handbook
  • Start working on basic knots and skills

Scout Rank Requirements

Your Scout rank covers the essential knowledge every Scout needs. Here’s what you’ll learn and demonstrate:

Scouting Knowledge

  • Scout Oath, Law, Motto, and Slogan: Learn these by heart and explain what they mean to you
  • Scout sign, salute, and handshake: Understand when and how to use each one properly
  • Patrol method: Learn how Scouts work together in small groups
  • Scout advancement: Understand how you progress through ranks and earn merit badges
  • Scout uniform: Know how to wear your uniform properly with pride

Basic Skills

  • Knots: Show how to tie a square knot and two half-hitches - your first practical outdoor skills
  • Pocketknife safety: Demonstrate safe handling and the “blood circle” safety rule
  • Citizenship: Explain the Pledge of Allegiance and tell about the American flag
  • Cyber safety: Complete online training to learn safe internet practices

Scout Participation

  • Scoutmaster conference: A friendly one-on-one chat with your Scoutmaster about what you’ve learned
  • Board of review: Meet with adult leaders to share your Scout knowledge and experience

Requirements Process: How Advancement Works

Understanding how to get your requirements signed off is important for your success in Scouting:

  • Initially, Scouts get their rank requirements signed off by more advanced Scouts
  • Scouts should bring their Scout Handbook to meetings and outings for sign-offs
  • Scout’s name MUST be inside and outside their book
  • Scouts need a pencil or pen and small notebook for taking notes and tracking progress
  • Make photocopies or take pictures of completed pages every 2-3 months in case the book is lost
  • Unlike in Cub Scouts, parents may not sign off on requirements

Important: Your Scout Handbook is your advancement record - take good care of it and keep it with you at meetings and activities!

Your Scout Family

During your first month, you’ll meet the people who will support your Scouting journey:

Your Patrol Leader

  • An experienced Scout (usually 14-16 years old) who leads your patrol
  • Your primary mentor and guide during meetings and activities

Your Scoutmaster

  • The adult leader who oversees the troop and your advancement

Merit Badge Counselors

  • Adult experts who will help you explore your interests through merit badges
  • Each counselor specializes in different subject areas
  • You’ll work with them to complete merit badge requirements at your own pace

Your Scout Buddy

  • An experienced Scout assigned to help you during your first few months
  • Someone to sit with during meetings and help you feel included
  • Available to answer questions about troop procedures and activities

Knowledge and Skills

Connections and Belonging

Advancement to First Class

Welcome to your 18-month journey from Scout to First Class! This is where you’ll learn the essential skills that will serve you throughout your entire Scouting adventure. Advancement in Scouting America provides a progressive series of challenges that help Scouts develop skills, confidence, and leadership abilities.

The advancement path is outlined in the Scout Handbook. Scouts can work on any requirement for advancement or merit badges at any time. However, they can only advance a rank when ALL requirements for that rank are completed. Rank advancement is earned by each scout; rank advancement is not automatic.

Your Journey: Scout to First Class

The ranks from Scout to First Class (Scout → Tenderfoot → Second Class → First Class) teach you all the fundamental skills you’ll need on your Scouting journey. While we encourage Scouts to work toward First Class within their first 18 months, this is a goal, not a requirement. Every Scout progresses at their own pace, and that’s perfectly okay!

Skills You’ll Master

Outdoor Skills

  • Camping and outdoor ethics (Leave No Trace principles)
  • Cooking skills and camp safety
  • First aid and emergency preparedness
  • Navigation using map and compass
  • Knots and rope work
  • Fire building and outdoor cooking
  • Weather awareness and preparation

Physical Fitness

  • Personal fitness planning and tracking
  • Swimming and water safety
  • Hiking and backpacking basics

Leadership and Citizenship

  • Leadership roles within your patrol
  • Community service projects
  • Citizenship and civic responsibility
  • Communication and teamwork skills

Why First Class matters: Scouts who earn First Class within their first 18 months are much more likely to stay in Scouting and eventually earn their Eagle Scout rank. These skills give you the confidence and ability to participate fully in all our outdoor adventures and leadership opportunities.

First Class Rank Progression

Advancement Order: Scout → Tenderfoot → Second Class → First Class

Timeline and Requirements Overview

  • Scout: Your introduction to Scouting (first 30 days)
  • Tenderfoot: Basic outdoor skills and Scout knowledge (typically 2-4 months after Scout)
  • Second Class: Intermediate camping, cooking, and outdoor skills (typically 6-8 months after Scout)
  • First Class: Advanced outdoor skills, leadership, and citizenship (typically 12-18 months after Scout)

Note: These are typical timelines. Every Scout progresses at their own pace based on their participation, effort, and opportunities to complete requirements.

Merit Badge Process

  • Complete requirements during troop meetings, camp, or individual study

Merit Badge Opportunities

Advancement to Eagle

The Eagle Scout rank is Scouting’s highest achievement, representing years of growth, leadership, and service. After earning First Class, your Scouting journey shifts from learning basic skills to developing expertise, leadership, and making a lasting impact on your community.

The Journey: Scout to Eagle

Foundation Ranks (Ages 11-14)

Scout → Tenderfoot → Second Class → First Class

  • Focus on learning fundamental outdoor skills
  • Develop basic leadership and citizenship understanding
  • Build confidence through progressive challenges
  • Establish the foundation for all future Scouting experiences

Advanced Ranks (Ages 14+)

Star → Life → Eagle

  • Emphasis shifts to leadership, service, and personal growth
  • Merit badge requirements become more diverse and challenging
  • Leadership positions require greater responsibility and longer tenure
  • Community service hours increase significantly

Star Scout Requirements

Merit Badges: Earn 6 merit badges (4 must be from Eagle-required list) Leadership: Serve actively in a leadership position for 4 months Service: Complete 6 hours of community service Skills: Demonstrate Scout spirit and outdoor skills proficiency

Life Scout Requirements

Merit Badges: Earn 5 additional merit badges (3 must be from Eagle-required list) Leadership: Serve actively in a leadership position for 6 months Service: Complete 6 hours of community service Skills: Plan, develop, and lead a service project

Eagle Scout Requirements

Merit Badge Requirements

Total: 21 merit badges (including 13 specific Eagle-required badges)

Eagle-Required Merit Badges (All 13 Required):

  1. First Aid - Emergency response and medical skills
  2. Citizenship in the Community - Local civic engagement
  3. Citizenship in the Nation - National civic responsibility
  4. Citizenship in the World - Global awareness and responsibility
  5. Communication - Written and verbal communication skills
  6. Personal Fitness - Physical health and wellness
  7. Emergency Preparedness OR Lifesaving - Crisis response skills
  8. Environmental Science OR Sustainability - Environmental stewardship
  9. Personal Management - Financial literacy and life skills
  10. Swimming OR Hiking OR Cycling - Physical fitness activity
  11. Camping - Advanced outdoor skills
  12. Cooking - Food preparation and nutrition
  13. Family Life - Relationship and family skills

Additional Merit Badges: 8 merit badges of your choice from any available options

The Eagle Scout Service Project

The Eagle project is the capstone of your Scouting experience and what often distinguishes Eagle Scouts as leaders throughout their lives.

Project Requirements

  • Benefits: Must help a religious institution, school, or community organization (not a Scouting unit)
  • Leadership: You must lead the project - others can help, but you’re in charge
  • Planning: Develop detailed project proposal, timeline, and budget
  • Approval: Get approval from your troop, beneficiary, and council before starting
  • Documentation: Keep detailed records of planning, execution, and results

Project Process

  1. Identify a Need: Work with organizations to find meaningful projects
  2. Develop Your Proposal: Create detailed plans, timelines, and resource requirements
  3. Get Approvals: Present your proposal to troop committee, beneficiary, and council
  4. Execute Your Project: Lead volunteers to complete the work
  5. Document Results: Complete final reports and documentation
  6. Present to Board of Review: Demonstrate your leadership and project impact

Project Ideas

  • Build outdoor classrooms or gardens for schools
  • Create accessibility improvements for community centers
  • Develop educational trails or outdoor learning spaces
  • Construct storage solutions for food banks or shelters
  • Organize community resource fairs or educational programs
  • Design and build playground equipment or outdoor fitness areas

Leadership Skills Developed

  • Project Management: Planning, budgeting, scheduling, and execution
  • Team Leadership: Motivating and directing volunteers
  • Communication: Presenting ideas, writing proposals, and coordinating with organizations
  • Problem Solving: Adapting to challenges and finding creative solutions
  • Service Leadership: Understanding community needs and creating lasting impact

The Eagle Scout Legacy

Eagle Scout is more than a rank - it’s a commitment to lifelong leadership and service. Eagle Scouts are found among leaders in every field: business, government, military, education, and community service. The skills you develop earning Eagle - leadership, project management, service, and personal character - will serve you throughout your life.

Your journey to Eagle starts with building strong foundations in your first year. Focus on learning, growing, and developing the skills that will carry you through this incredible adventure. The Eagle path will be there when you’re ready - and the journey itself is just as valuable as the destination.

Remember: Only about 4% of all Scouts earn Eagle Scout. It requires dedication, persistence, and commitment - but the rewards last a lifetime. Every Eagle Scout will tell you: the journey changes you, and the rank opens doors you never imagined.

While Scouting America is youth-led, it requires dedicated adult volunteers who undergo comprehensive training in youth protection, position-specific skills, advanced leadership development, and event/safety-specific training. We encourage any and all parents to become trained leaders, but we also realize that not everyone has the capacity in their lives at this time to take on formal leadership roles.

Support Your Scout

The best way to get involved is by helping your Scout do their best in the Scouting program. Remember, help and support does not mean do it for them. A key tenet of Scouting is to develop independent and capable leaders. As Lord Baden-Powell said, “Never do for a Scout what they can do for themselves.”

Every Scout develops on their own timeline, and the level of support needed varies from Scout to Scout and changes over time. Some Scouts may need more guidance with organization and reminders, while others quickly become self-directed. What works for one Scout may not work for another, and that’s completely normal! Talk to Scout leaders and other parents to learn more about everyone’s Scouting journey - their experiences can provide valuable insights into supporting your own Scout’s growth and development.

Here are ways to support your Scout’s success:

  • Remind them to follow communications and schedules by reviewing Scoutbook and troop announcements together
  • Assist with getting their uniform together and ensuring they have required gear
  • Encourage them to take ownership of their advancement and merit badge progress
  • Support their leadership development by letting them solve problems and make decisions
  • Communicate with adult leaders about accessibility options or special considerations that help our leaders better understand how to work with and support your Scout along their Scouting journey
  • Be available for transportation when they need to attend meetings and activities
  • Help with setup and cleanup at events while letting your Scout take the lead on their responsibilities

Advocate for Your Scout’s Needs Our troop provides an accessible and inclusive environment for every eligible youth. We will always do our best to support your scouts needs, but you can help our best be better. We require a joining meeting prior to your Scout’s first overnight outing, but that’s not the end of our discussion. You are encouraged to request a pre-event check-in with leaders attending any event (particularly helpful for summer camp) to ensure we can provide the best possible support for your Scout’s success.

Troop Leadership

Troop 500G operates under the Scouting America troop leadership model, where youth leaders are elected by the troop and adult leaders provide guidance and support to all youth-led activities.

The Patrol Method

A Scout patrol is a small team of normally six to eight members where Scouts learn skills together, share responsibilities and take on leadership roles. Each patrol elects its own patrol leader and develops its own identity and traditions.

“The patrol system is not one method in which Scouting can be carried on. It is the only method.” —Lord Baden-Powell, Scouting’s founder

For more information about the patrol method, see the Scouting America Patrol Method Guide.

Youth Leadership Positions

Troop 500G Leadership Structure and Organization

Senior Patrol Leader (SPL)

The highest youth leadership position, elected by the troop. With three green bars behind the Scout emblem, the senior patrol leader’s shoulder patch symbolizes one of the oldest leadership positions in Scouting. Scouting America has long recognized the senior patrol leader as the highest youth leadership position in a troop. They are the primary link between a troop’s Scouts and its adult leaders. They shoulder the responsibility for leading meetings of the troop and the patrol leaders’ council and provide valuable leadership in planning and carrying out the troop’s program of outdoor activities, service projects, and events.

Senior Patrol Leader Responsibilities:

  • Runs all troop meetings, events, activities, and the annual program planning conference
  • Runs the patrol leaders’ council (PLC) meetings
  • Appoints other troop youth leaders with the advice and counsel of the Scoutmaster
  • Assigns duties and responsibilities to youth leaders
  • Assists the Scoutmaster with youth leader training
  • Sets a good example
  • Wears the Scout uniform correctly
  • Lives by the Scout Oath and Law
  • Shows Scout spirit

For more information about the Senior Patrol Leader role, see the Scouting America Senior Patrol Leader Guide and refer to Scouting America Publication Senior Patrol Leader Handbook.

Patrol Leaders

Lead individual patrols, elected by patrol members. The patrol leader is the patrol’s key leader, representing the patrol at all patrol leaders’ council meetings and the annual program planning conference, and keeping patrol members informed of decisions made. Patrol leaders carry out planning, leading, and evaluating patrol meetings and activities, and assure patrols are prepared to participate in all troop activities. They keep their patrol intact so they can work together and share responsibilities to get things done. It is incumbent upon them to be a good example for the members of their patrol and the rest of the troop.

Patrol Leader Responsibilities:

  • Plans and leads patrol meetings and activities
  • Keeps patrol members informed
  • Assigns each patrol member a specific duty
  • Represents his patrol at all patrol leaders’ council meetings and the annual program planning conference
  • Prepares the patrol to participate in all troop activities
  • Works with other troop leaders to make the troop run well
  • Knows the abilities of each patrol member
  • Sets a good example
  • Wears the Scout uniform correctly
  • Lives by the Scout Oath and Law
  • Shows and develops patrol spirit

For more information about the Patrol Leader role, see the Scouting America Patrol Leader Guide and refer to Scouting America Publication The Patrol Leader Handbook.

Quartermaster

Quartermasters serve as the troop’s supply boss. They keep an inventory of troop equipment and see that the gear is in good condition. They work with patrol quartermasters as they check out equipment and return it. At meetings of the patrol leaders’ council they report on the status of equipment in need of replacement or repair. In carrying out their responsibilities, they may have the guidance of a member of the troop committee.

Quartermaster Responsibilities:

  • Keeps records on patrol and troop equipment
  • Makes sure equipment is in good working condition
  • Issues equipment and makes sure it is returned in good condition
  • Makes suggestions for new or replacement items
  • Works with the troop committee member responsible for equipment
  • Sets a good example
  • Wears the Scout uniform correctly
  • Lives by the Scout Oath and Law
  • Shows Scout spirit

For more information about the Quartermaster role, see the Scouting America Quartermaster Guide.

Scribe

The scribe is the troop’s secretary. Though not a voting member, they attend meetings of the patrol leaders’ council and keep a record of the discussions. They cooperate with the patrol scribes to record attendance at troop meetings and to maintain troop advancement records. The troop scribe may be assisted by a member of the troop committee.

Scribe Responsibilities:

  • Attends and keeps a log of patrol leaders’ council meetings
  • Records individual Scout attendance
  • Records individual Scout advancement progress
  • Works with the troop committee member responsible for records and finance
  • Sets a good example
  • Wears the Scout uniform correctly
  • Lives by the Scout Oath and Law
  • Shows Scout spirit

For more information about the Scribe role, see the Scouting America Scribe Guide.

Annual Planning

Elections and Leadership Transitions

Senior Patrol Leader Elections

The Senior Patrol Leader (SPL) is elected by the troop every six months. Candidates must meet the following eligibility requirements:

  • Hold a rank of First Class or higher
  • Have been active in a Scout troop for at least six months

Patrol and Troop Position Elections

Patrol Leaders are elected by their patrol members and other troop positions are appointed by the SPL with the advice and counsel of the Scoutmaster. Experienced Scouts holding a rank of First Class or higher, and having been active in a Scout troop for at least six months, will have an opportunity to run for patrol and troop positions.

Elections provide Scouts with real-world experience in democratic processes and give every eligible Scout the opportunity to serve in a leadership role.

Patrol Leaders’ Council (PLC)

Those elected to leadership positions are expected to participate in Patrol Leaders’ Conferences (PLC), which are held at least monthly. The PLC is the troop’s planning and coordination body, led by the Senior Patrol Leader.

Purpose and Benefits

The joining conference builds trust and rapport between parents and leaders as partners in delivering the Scouting program. It helps us understand each Scout’s unique strengths, interests, and any special considerations that will help ensure their success in our program.

Key Benefits:

  • Establishes open communication between families and leadership
  • Helps leaders understand each Scout’s individual strengths and needs
  • Creates a supportive environment where every Scout can thrive
  • Builds trust and partnership between parents and troop leadership

  • Sets expectations for ongoing communication

When and Where

Policies & Safety

Safety is our top priority in all troop activities. We follow Scouting America’s Guide to Safe Scouting and maintain additional troop-specific policies.

Youth Protection / Safeguarding Youth

All adult leaders complete Youth Protection Training before participating in troop activities. We maintain two-deep leadership on all activities and follow all Youth Protection guidelines.

For more information, visit: Scouting America Youth Protection

Youth Protection Training Requirements

All scout leaders are trained and certified in the Youth Protection program through Scouting America. Scout parents are also required to complete a Youth Protection exercise with their daughter and submit documentation to the troop.

Two Deep Leadership

To ensure the safety of all scouts, Scouting America requires “Two Deep Leadership” - at least two adults must be present for any scouting activity. This is especially essential during outings, summer camp, and high adventure activities. To achieve this level of safety, parents must take an active part in the operations and activities of the troop.

Mandatory Report of Child Abuse

All persons involved in Scouting shall report to local authorities any good-faith suspicion or belief that any child is or has been physically or sexually abused, physically or emotionally neglected, exposed to any form of violence or threat, exposed to any form of sexual exploitation, including the possession, manufacture, or distribution of child pornography, online solicitation, enticement, or showing of obscene material. You may not abdicate this reporting responsibility to any other person.

Scouts First Helpline

As part of its “Scouts First” approach to the protection and safety of youth, Scouting America has established a dedicated 24-hour helpline to receive reports of known or suspected abuse or behavior that might put a youth at risk. 1-844-SCOUTS1 (1-844-726-8871) If immediate assistance is needed in the handling of a sexual abuse allegation, contact Scouts First Helpline (1-844-SCOUTS1). If someone is at immediate risk of harm, always call 911.

Overview

Every Scout who joins Troop 500G brings their own unique strengths, interests, and needs — and this conference is our chance to learn about yours. By taking a little time to talk before your Scout’s first campout, we can make sure our leaders are prepared to support them from day one. This form is a guide for that conversation, not an evaluation — there are no wrong answers, and you are always welcome to leave any question blank. Everything shared here stays confidential within our leadership team and is used only to help your Scout thrive.

A Note About Scout Leadership

Troop 500G leaders are volunteers — mostly parents who care deeply about Scouts and want to help. We attend a lot of training, but we also have full-time jobs and busy lives. Sometimes we miss details or make mistakes, but our goal is always to support your Scout and help them succeed. Just as Scouts “Do their best,” we do too. If you ever have concerns or questions, please talk to us. We’ll figure it out together!

A Conversation Guide

The Lake Erie Council Accessibility and Inclusion Committee encourages Units to hold joining conferences with every incoming Scout. These meetings are between leaders (often Scoutmasters, Assistant Scoutmasters, and New Member Coordinators), parents, and Scouts. Joining meetings encourage inclusive, strengths-based conversations to better understand and support each Scout’s unique needs. The conference should take place within the first month after a Scout joins, and is designed to build partnerships with families rather than evaluate suitability.

Inclusive

Joining conferences are held for every Scout, recognizing that all Scouts have unique needs and strengths. Some may require additional support, but every Scout benefits from understanding and partnership.

Confidentiality & Privacy

The goal of joining conferences is to provide leaders enough information to support Scouts during camping and troop activities. While confidentiality is important, we ask that parents and guardians share enough information with our trained leaders to help their Scout succeed. Required Annual Health and Medical Forms store private medical information and are not authorized for digital storage. All joining conference information is stored in Troop 500G Google Drive, with access limited to adult troop leaders who need it to provide support to your Scout. If you have privacy concerns, please let us know. You may leave any fields blank if you prefer not to share certain information. All details discussed are kept confidential unless explicit permission is given to share with other leaders.

You’re part of the team

Leaders, parents, and Scouts work together as a team, and the goal is a supportive partnership where every Scout can thrive. Open communication is essential — if something is worrying you, please say so. If something is unclear, just ask. We would rather hear about a concern early than have a Scout struggle in silence.

Strengths-Based

Every Scout brings their own unique strengths, talents, and interests. The joining conference focuses on identifying and building upon these strengths, helping Scouts feel confident and valued in the troop. That said, building on strengths is most effective when leaders also understand where a Scout might struggle — knowing potential challenges helps us provide the right support before small difficulties become bigger ones.

No Diagnosis Required

The conversation is about what helps the Scout succeed — not about medical diagnoses or labels. Families are encouraged to share strategies, preferences, and needs, regardless of whether there is a formal diagnosis.

Conducted Post-Joining

The joining conference is held after the Scout has joined the troop. It is not an evaluation or selection process, but a way to welcome the Scout and ensure they have the support they need from the start.

An Ongoing Conversation

The joining conference is just the beginning of a supportive relationship. Scouts, parents, and leaders all grow and change, and so do their needs. Any leader, Scout, or parent may request another conference at any time to revisit goals, address new challenges, or update support strategies. We especially encourage a follow-up conference with leaders and families before Scouts attend summer camp, to ensure everyone is prepared and supported for new experiences.

Independent and Regional Camps

Camp Tuscazoar

Founded more than a century ago, Camp Tuscazoar is now operated by the Camp Tuscazoar Foundation as an Ohio non-profit corporation. This historic camp continues to serve Scouts and youth organizations while also welcoming weekend campers, hikers, mountain bikers, and horse riders throughout the year.

Laurel Highlands High Adventure

A high adventure destination we’re interested in visiting in the future. Laurel Highlands offers exciting outdoor adventure programs and activities that provide Scouts with opportunities to challenge themselves and develop confidence in wilderness settings.

Local Outdoor Adventure Partners

Camp Hi Adventure

A privately-owned outdoor adventure facility on the Cuyahoga River, operated by the Hazlett family since 1928. Our troop has historically participated in family canoe and kayak events following the annual Twins Day Festival. With over 250 canoes, kayaks, and paddle boards, Camp Hi provides “Adventure in Your Own Backyard” experiences that promote the restorative benefits of river recreation.

National High Adventure Bases

Philmont Scout Ranch

Our Scouts and Scouters have participated in treks at Philmont Scout Ranch, Scouting America’s premier high adventure base in New Mexico. This 140,000-acre wilderness ranch offers life-changing backpacking experiences that challenge Scouts physically, mentally, and spiritually while building leadership skills and lifelong memories.

Sea Base

Scouting America’s aquatic high adventure base in the Florida Keys offers unique maritime adventures including sailing, snorkeling, scuba diving, and marine conservation programs in some of the most beautiful waters in the world.

Northern Tier

Located in Minnesota’s Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness, Northern Tier provides canoe and backpacking adventures in pristine wilderness settings, offering Scouts the opportunity to experience untouched nature and develop self-reliance skills.

Camp Selection Process

Our troop chooses different camps each summer based on several factors:

  • Scout Preferences: We let our Scouts’ interests drive our camp selection
  • Program Offerings: Different camps excel in different areas (aquatics, high adventure, traditional skills, etc.)
  • New Experiences: We like to expose our Scouts to different camp environments and programs
  • Quality and Safety: All selected camps maintain high standards for safety and program quality

Planning Your Camp Experience

Before Camp:

  • Visit camp websites to learn about their programs and facilities
  • Check what merit badges and activities will be available
  • Prepare by reviewing Scout skills and requirements

During Camp:

  • Participate fully in all activities and programs
  • Work on advancement opportunities
  • Build friendships with Scouts from other troops
  • Embrace new challenges and experiences

After Camp:

  • Share your experiences with the troop
  • Complete any follow-up requirements for merit badges
  • Apply skills learned at camp to future troop activities
